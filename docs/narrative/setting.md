# Wayfarer — Setting Bible

## Concept

**Wayfarer** is a cosmic fantasy RPG about two companions wandering between planes of existence, following the trail of unintended consequences left by an antagonist who doesn't know the damage they're causing. It is slow, thoughtful, and grounded. Technology is minimal. Combat is swords and skill. Wonder is the primary emotion.

Tone references: *Blade of the Immortal*, *Disco Elysium*, *Pillars of Eternity*, *Nausicaä of the Valley of the Wind*, *Planescape: Torment*

---

## The Veil

The planes of existence have always touched — thin places called **the Veil** where one world bleeds into another. For most of history, Veil-tears were rare, controlled, sacred. Now they are erupting everywhere: in rice paddies, in cattle pastures, in the middle of quiet towns. Unstable wormholes swallowing whole fields. Nobody knows why.

The portals are not neutral. They respond to **conviction** — the emotional clarity and belief of whoever attempts transit. A person without it gets torn apart crossing over. This is why most people fear them and few can use them. The mechanism isn't fully understood; some religious orders call it faith, some scholars call it resonance, some say the Veil simply knows.

Something upstream is wrong. Something is destabilizing the Veil network. The companions are following the ripples.

## The Companions

### Sarro *(working name — the Manji figure)*

**God-touched. Searching for something lost.**

Sarro is nephilim — half mortal, half divine, born of the quasi-Roman **New Pantheon** that rose after the last great Veil collapse centuries ago. The gods of the New Pantheon are not ancient and mystical; they are relatively recent, born from belief crystallized during catastrophe, and they have mortal descendants scattered across the planes.

His divine blood means he passes through Veil-tears without harm. He does not need conviction; the portals simply recognize him. This makes him invaluable — and also means he's been drifting through planes for years without any plane ever quite feeling like home.

He is not searching for glory or purpose. He lost something specific — a person, a place, a memory, a name — and the planes keep not having it. He fights well and effortlessly, which makes it easy to not think about what he's looking for.

**Opening:** We meet him mid-brawl in a roadside tavern. Capable, slightly reckless, clearly not trying very hard. He wins without much concern.

---

### Liris *(working name — the Rin figure)*

**Mortal. Passionate about something that matters to her.**

Liris is from a farming community — the kind of place that doesn't think much about the Veil or the New Pantheon. She has her own thing she cares deeply about (to be developed: a craft, a person, a cause, something she's trying to reach or protect). She is not a fighter. She is not naive. She is patient and warm and occasionally furious.

**Opening:** After the tavern, we find Liris at home. Slow and peaceful — feeding animals, tending her barn, existing contentedly in the particular way of someone who belongs somewhere. Then Sarro, fleeing or stumbling out of the aftermath of the brawl, accidentally burns her barn. (The fire spreads from a knocked lantern, a skirmish that spilled out of the tavern, whatever fits — it's his fault.)

He can't give her money he doesn't have. He can't rebuild it before the season turns. What he *can* do is get her where she needs to go, through whatever portals open. He pledges it — somewhat awkwardly, sincerely. She accepts, somewhat skeptically.

They do not immediately like each other. That's the point.

## The Antagonist(s)

**Core truth:** The Veil is a living wound trying to heal itself. Every intervention — however well-intentioned — tears it open again. The antagonists are not villains. They are people trying hard to fix something, and that effort is exactly what's breaking it.

The resolution of the game is not a fight. It's convincing them to *stop*. To trust that the Veil can heal if left alone. That is harder than any battle.

### The Menders *(working faction name)*

A loose network of scholars, engineers, and true believers who have diagnosed the Veil crisis and are actively attempting to repair it using forbidden techniques — devices that force tears open, methods that map Veil structure from the inside, rituals meant to reinforce weak points. Each intervention leaves the area worse. They don't see it because they move on before the consequences fully manifest. The companions arrive into the aftermath.

The Menders are earnest and sympathetic. Some are brilliant. Some are exhausted and scared. They are not acting from greed — they genuinely believe the Veil will collapse entirely if no one acts.

### The Extractors *(second faction — to develop later)*

Where the Menders want to fix the Veil, a second faction wants to *use* it. A more mercantile, capitalist interest — harvesting Veil energy for power, profit, or leverage. Their damage is collateral and they largely don't care. Less sympathetic than the Menders, but not cartoonishly evil — they've convinced themselves the extraction is sustainable.

These two factions may not know about each other, may be in conflict, or may have an uneasy arrangement. To be developed.

---

**The ripple loop (gameplay):**

An antagonist transits through a plane, or performs a Veil intervention there → bleedthrough event occurs (wrong creature appears, local water becomes something alien, people start dreaming in another language, a building briefly becomes somewhere else) → companions arrive to find the mess → solve the immediate problem → find the trail → move on.

The companions glimpse the antagonists occasionally — close enough to start to understand, not close enough to stop them. Sometimes their goals briefly align.

The antagonist is not the final boss. The question of *whether to stop them* is more interesting than the fight.

## Mechanical Pillars

1. **Exploration** — planes are small and handcrafted, but dense. You're meant to wander, talk, discover.
2. **Real-time-with-pause combat** — swords, not guns. D&D SRD-compatible rules reskinned for the setting.
3. **Portal as pacing mechanic** — you can't rush. To safely use a portal, something in the current plane must feel resolved. (Sarro passes freely; Liris may not.)
4. **Two-person party** — Sarro and Liris, both controllable. Their dynamic evolves through play.

No crafting. No inventory management as a minigame. Combat is meaningful but not the focus.

---

## What This Game Is Not

- Not sci-fi (despite planar travel)
- Not mass combat or strategy
- Not an open world
- Not a crafting game
- Not an MMO or persistent world
